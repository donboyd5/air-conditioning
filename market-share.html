7  Market share and share of growth

This chapter tackles the piece of the original brief not yet quantified: what share of the industry does each player hold, and — more important for investing — what share of the growth are they positioned to capture? The second question matters more than the first: a company with a modest overall share but a dominant position in the fast, high-margin pools (data-center cooling, services) can be a better investment than a volume leader stuck in the slow, low-margin pools.

WarningHow to read the share numbers

Market-share data here is genuinely fuzzy, for two reasons. Basis: revenue vs. units. Daikin leads on equipment revenue; Chinese makers (Midea, Gree) lead on units because of China’s enormous domestic volume. Scope: “air conditioning” vs. “HVAC” vs. regional cuts move a company’s share by several points. Most precise percentages come from paywalled secondary aggregators and should be treated as directional, not authoritative. The reputable primary anchors are JARN (units), BSRIA (paywalled), and company filings. I give ranges and flag the weakest figures.

7.1 Global share: a revenue leader and a volume leader

The single most important structural fact: there is no dominant global player, and “the leader” depends on what you measure. Daikin is #1 by equipment revenue; the Chinese makers lead by units.

Concentration: moderately concentrated at the top, fragmented in the tail. The top 5 hold roughly 45–55% combined, with no single firm above the mid-teens, and hundreds of regional players below the top ~10. Regionally, Asia-Pacific is ~47% of global HVAC revenue, and China is the single largest market by units (the global room-AC market was 140.6M units in 2024, +4%, per JARN).1

7.2 U.S. share: a consolidated oligopoly, now reshuffling

The U.S. market is more concentrated than the global one, and it is actively reshuffling: in August 2025, Johnson Controls completed the sale of its residential & light-commercial HVAC business to Bosch (~$8B), instantly turning Bosch into a major U.S. residential player and shrinking JCI to a commercial/applied + controls company.2

Note

Why the U.S. numbers don’t add up cleanly. A commonly cited stat is that Carrier + Trane + Lennox hold ~50–55% of the U.S. residential market — yet the individual estimates above, summed with Daikin and JCI, exceed 100%. The discrepancy is real and comes from inconsistent scope (residential-only vs. total HVAC; units vs. revenue; which year) across sources. The honest read: the U.S. is a consolidated oligopoly of ~5–6 brands (Carrier, Trane, Daikin/Goodman, Lennox, Rheem, and now Bosch), but the precise split is not reliably knowable from public secondary data. Note also that Watsco is the largest distributor (~18% of a fragmented ~$64B distribution market) — distribution share, not manufacturing.

7.3 Where the growth is: five pools

Overall industry growth (~6% in dollars) masks very different sub-pools. This chart is illustrative — the bases differ (units vs. dollars vs. cyclical), so read it as relative positioning, not precise forecasts.

Pool 1 — Data-center cooling (highest quality). The fastest, highest-margin pool. Liquid cooling alone is roughly $5–6B (2025) growing ~20–25% to ~$15–19B by 2030, driven by hyperscaler AI capex (~$380B in 2025). Captured disproportionately by Vertiv (clear leader), Modine (fastest grower off a small base — data-center revenue +119% in FY2025), nVent, and Schneider.3

Pool 2 — Heat pumps / electrification (high quality, cyclical). A large structural shift, but policy-driven and volatile: European sales fell ~22% in 2024, then began rebounding ~+10% in 2025. Captured by Daikin (~18–20% EU share), Carrier (via its ~€12B Viessmann acquisition), NIBE (purest listed play), and private Bosch/Vaillant.4

Pool 3 — Emerging-market first-time adoption (biggest volume, lowest quality). The largest unit-growth pool — India ~15M units (2025) heading toward ~28M by 2030 — but volume-led, thin-margin, and dominated by Gree, Midea, Haier, Daikin, LG/Samsung, plus India-listed Voltas, Blue Star. Lower-quality growth for Western investors.5

Pool 4 — U.S. replacement + ASP uplift (moderate volume, high value). Low unit growth but a high-value, replacement-driven base with a genuine ~8–10% price uplift from the refrigerant/efficiency transition. Captured by Trane, Carrier, Daikin/Goodman, Lennox, Rheem (carries the antitrust overhang from the regulation chapter).

Pool 5 — Service / aftermarket / controls (highest-margin recurring). Smaller headline growth but the highest-quality dollars — recurring, high-margin, installed-base-driven. Led by Trane (record $7.8B backlog at YE2025) and Johnson Controls (OpenBlue controls), with Carrier and Daikin growing their recurring mix.6

7.5 Share-of-growth ranking

Weighting toward pools that are large and high-quality (recurring/ASP/margin) rather than pure low-margin volume:

Tier 1 — most + highest-quality growth exposure. Vertiv (near-pure data-center cooling, the single best pool); Trane (U.S. replacement/ASP + best-in-class service/controls + heat-pump optionality); Carrier (biggest heat-pump bet + replacement + growing services).

Tier 2 — strong but narrower or higher-beta. Daikin (uniquely spans all five pools as global #1, but diluted by low-margin emerging-market volume); Modine (highest incremental data-center leverage off a small base); Johnson Controls (best recurring service/controls, weaker elsewhere).

Tier 3 — narrower or lower-quality. Lennox (clean but single-pool U.S. replacement); NIBE (purest heat-pump, single policy-driven pool, highest cycle beta); nVent (data-center participant, narrow); Gree/Midea/Haier (largest unit pool but lowest-margin, Asian-OEM-weighted).

Note

Investor takeaway. Overall market share and share of growth point to different names. By sheer size, the leaders are Daikin and the Chinese volume makers. But the highest share of high-quality growth sits with Vertiv (data center), Trane (replacement + services), and Carrier (heat pumps + replacement + services). Daikin has the broadest reach but the most diluted quality. The biggest unit growth — emerging-market adoption — accrues mostly to Chinese/Korean OEMs and is the lowest-quality pool from a margin standpoint. For your 10–15 year horizon, “share of high-quality growth” is the more useful lens than headline market share.

Open questions: (1) Want me to firm up any single share figure with a primary source (the cleanest would be commissioning BSRIA/JARN, which are paid — otherwise company filings give revenue but not clean share)? (2) Should the exposure matrix become the backbone of a scoring model that combines share of growth with the valuation data from the financials chapter — i.e., a simple “quality-of-growth vs. price” screen across the shortlist?
