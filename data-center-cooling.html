5  Data-center cooling: the fastest-growing cooling market

This is, by some distance, the highest-growth corner of “air conditioning broadly defined,” and it overlaps only partly with the residential/commercial HVAC names. It deserves its own chapter because the demand driver (AI compute), the technology (liquid cooling), and the beneficiaries (Vertiv, Modine, nVent, Schneider) are largely distinct from the broad-AC story.

5.1 Why it is growing: heat density, not floor space

The driver is rack power density. A traditional server rack drew ~10–15 kW; AI training and inference racks now run 30–50 kW commonly, and next-generation GPU racks are cited at 100–300+ kW. Air physically cannot remove heat at those densities, which forces a structural shift from air cooling to liquid cooling. So unlike the broad AC market (driven by climate and incomes), data-center cooling growth is driven by the AI capital-expenditure cycle — faster, but also more cyclical and more concentrated among a handful of hyperscale customers.

5.2 Air vs. liquid — and what is winning within liquid

The reality is hybrid: direct-to-chip liquid for the hottest GPUs, air or rear-door exchangers for the rest, immersion in select niches.

Direct-to-chip (cold plate) — the near-term winner. Coolant is piped to metal plates sitting on the CPU/GPU. It is the fastest-growing mainstream method, cited at roughly 43–47% of liquid-cooling share in 2025, and is favored in NVIDIA’s reference designs.

Rear-door heat exchangers (RDHx) — transitional. Liquid-cooled panels on the back of the rack; used as a retrofit/bridge and in hybrid halls, but limited at the highest densities.

Immersion cooling — high efficiency, slower adoption. Servers submerged in a non-conductive (dielectric) fluid. The highest projected growth rate (some estimates ~26–34% CAGR) but off a small base, held back by serviceability, fluid cost, and (for two-phase) PFAS concerns.

CDUs (coolant distribution units) — the indispensable plumbing. Every liquid deployment needs CDUs to move coolant between the facility loop and the server loops. Strong attach-rate growth across all methods, making CDU makers a relatively method-agnostic way to play liquid cooling.

5.5 Vertiv (VRT) profile

What it does. Vertiv designs, makes, and services critical digital-infrastructure equipment: critical power (UPS, switchgear, busway), thermal management (precision air cooling and, increasingly, liquid cooling — cold plates, CDUs, heat rejection), integrated rack/modular systems, and lifecycle services. It carries the Liebert cooling and Geist/NetSure power brands, and traces to Emerson Network Power.

Scale and momentum (FY2025). Net sales ~$10.2B, +27.7% year over year; operating margin ~17.9%; free cash flow ~$1.9B (FCF conversion ~142%). Most striking is the order book: backlog ~$15.0B (up ~109%), with Q4 2025 organic orders up ~252% and a book-to-bill near 2.9× — roughly 12–18 months of forward revenue visibility. Management guides ~27–29% organic revenue growth for 2026.2

How cooling-focused is it? Thermal management is roughly ~30% of revenue (with power ~32%, services ~23%, IT/integrated the rest), but Vertiv does not break out a precise cooling-vs-power split, and liquid cooling is described as the fastest-growing, highest-margin part of the mix. So Vertiv is not a building-HVAC company — it is an AI-data-center infrastructure company whose cooling franchise is a major, fast-growing slice.

Competitive position and risk. Vertiv is the leading scaled “one-stop” vendor combining power + thermal + services for AI data centers — broader than pure-play coolers (CoolIT, Boyd) and more cooling-deep than power-only peers (Eaton). It has been deepening the liquid-cooling/services moat through acquisitions (PurgeRite, Strategic Thermal Labs, ThermoKey). The risks are the flip side of the thesis: the valuation is rich (see the financial chapter — ~49× EV/EBITDA, beta ~2.0), earnings are tied to the AI-capex cycle, customer concentration is high among a few hyperscalers, and competition (Schneider/Motivair, nVent, Modine) could compress liquid-cooling margins over time.

Note

Investor takeaway. Data-center cooling is the place where genuinely new technology (liquid cooling) maps onto the fastest revenue growth — and it answers your “newer technology = bigger growth” question more clearly than the residential side. But it is an AI-capex play with AI-capex risk: high growth, high multiples, high cyclicality, concentrated customers. Vertiv is the flagship; Modine and nVent are the higher-purity cooling/CDU adjacencies; the cleanest pure-plays (Boyd, CoolIT) are private.

Open question: Do you want Modine (MOD) and nVent (NVT) added to the financial comp table alongside Vertiv, so the three liquid-cooling listed names sit side by side? And should immersion-cooling startups be tracked as a private-market watch list, or ignored as un-investable?
