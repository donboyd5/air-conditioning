12  Synthesis: watchlist and open questions

12.1 The thesis in one paragraph

Cooling demand is one of the largest, most durable, climate-and-income-driven growth stories available, with the global AC stock projected to grow roughly 3.5× by 2050 (International Energy Agency, 2018). On top of that secular base sits a cyclical equipment business that is being accelerated by regulation — rising efficiency standards (SEER2) and the forced low-GWP refrigerant transition (AIM Act) — which raises selling prices, pulls forward replacement, and penalizes low-tech competitors (U.S. Department of Energy, Energy Star, 2023; U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, 2024). The investing task is to own that growth through companies where AC is a large, high-quality share of the business, at a price that doesn’t already fully reflect it.

12.3 What would change the picture (risks)

  • Valuation. The best businesses (Trane, Vertiv, Daikin’s quality peers) rarely look cheap — quality and AI growth are at least partly priced in. Lennox and Daikin screen cheapest.
  • Antitrust overhang. The 2026 HVAC price-fixing suits against Carrier, Trane, Lennox, Daikin and others put the “sticky ASP uplift” thesis at some risk; all defendants deny the claims, but it’s unresolved. See the regulation chapter.
  • AI-capex cyclicality. The data-center cooling names (VRT/MOD/NVT) are levered to a concentrated set of hyperscaler customers and the AI capital cycle — high beta in both directions.
  • TFA / HFO regulation. The refrigerant-chemicals thesis (SOLS/CC) carries a long-tail risk that HFOs themselves face future restriction as TFA scrutiny grows.
  • Cyclical air-pocket. The 2024 pre-buy / 2025 shipment decline is near-term noise for a 10–15 year horizon, but could create a cheaper entry point worth exploiting.
  • China and tariffs; commodity low-end. Volume leaders (Midea/Gree/Haier) and supply chains carry policy risk; window/portable units are commoditized — don’t mistake volume leadership for profit leadership.
