1  What air conditioning is, and its main types

Air conditioning moves heat from inside a space to outside it, using a refrigerant that is compressed and expanded in a cycle (the vapor-compression cycle). The same cycle, run in reverse, is what a heat pump uses to bring heat into a building — which is why the cooling and heating industries are converging, and why “air conditioning, broadly defined” increasingly means “heat pumps.”

For an investor, the system type matters because it determines who the customer is, how the product is sold and installed, the price point, and which manufacturers compete. The major categories are below.

TipUnfamiliar terms?

Words like compressor, condenser, coil, air handler, inverter, VRF, and refrigerant are defined in plain language in the Glossary. In brief: every air conditioner runs the same vapor-compression cycle — a compressor (the “engine”) pumps a refrigerant fluid around a loop; the refrigerant absorbs heat indoors at the evaporator/indoor coil and dumps it outdoors at the condenser (the outdoor box). A fan/air handler blows room air across the coil to actually cool the space, and in a ducted home the furnace/air handler also pushes that air through the ducts.

1.1 Residential and light-commercial system types

Window and portable units. Self-contained boxes that cool a single room. Cheap, sold through retail, low margin, and dominated by Asian volume manufacturers (Midea, Gree, Haier) plus retail brands. Low technological moat.

Central / ducted split systems. The dominant U.S. residential format: an outdoor condenser plus an indoor coil and furnace/air handler, distributing cooled air through ducts. This is the heart of the North American market and where Carrier, Trane, Lennox, Daikin (Goodman/Amana), and Rheem compete.

Ductless mini-splits. An outdoor unit connected to one or more wall- or ceiling-mounted indoor heads, with no ductwork. Efficient, increasingly used for heating as well as cooling, and the format in which Daikin and Mitsubishi Electric are especially strong. Fast-growing in North America from a small base.

Packaged units (rooftop units, “RTUs”). Single-cabinet systems common on small commercial buildings (strip malls, big-box stores). A large, replacement-driven U.S. market.

1.2 Commercial and applied system types

VRF (variable refrigerant flow). Networks of indoor units served by a common outdoor system, with refrigerant flow modulated per zone. Efficient and flexible for mid-size commercial buildings; Daikin and Mitsubishi Electric pioneered it and lead it.

Chillers and applied systems. Large central plants (chillers, air handlers, cooling towers) for offices, hospitals, campuses, and data centers — the fastest-growing commercial sub-segment as AI drives data-center construction. This is the domain of Carrier, Trane, Johnson Controls, and Daikin’s Applied business, and increasingly a focus for LG and others.

District cooling and process/industrial cooling. Centralized cooling distributed to many buildings, plus refrigeration and process cooling for industry and the cold chain.

Note

Investor takeaway. The retail end (window units) is high-volume, low-margin, and commoditized. The value tends to concentrate in (1) ducted residential replacement in North America, (2) ductless/VRF where efficiency and zoning command a premium, and (3) applied/commercial and data-center cooling, where engineering content, service revenue, and recurring aftermarket are highest. Where a company sits on this spectrum largely explains its margins and multiple — a theme developed in the company chapters.
