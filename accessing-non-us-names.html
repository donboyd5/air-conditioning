Accessing non-US names (US brokerage)

Several of the most interesting names in this notebook are not ordinary U.S.-listed stocks. This appendix records the practical routes a U.S. investor (e.g., through Charles Schwab) can use, the trade-offs, and the correct tickers. It is factual mechanics, not investment advice, and brokerage offerings change — verify current availability and symbols with your broker before acting.

Three routes for a U.S. investor

  1. Ordinary U.S. listing. The simplest case — a normal U.S.-exchange ticker bought in your regular brokerage account in USD. Applies to Carrier (CARR), Trane (TT), Lennox (LII), Vertiv (VRT), Modine (MOD), nVent (NVT), Solstice (SOLS), Chemours (CC).

  2. U.S. OTC ADR / foreign-ordinary. Many foreign companies have an American Depositary Receipt (ADR) or foreign-ordinary share that trades over-the-counter (OTC) in USD, buyable in a normal U.S. account. Convenient, but these are often unsponsored, thinly traded with wide bid-ask spreads, may carry ADR custody/pass-through fees, can lag the home-market price, and offer less liquidity than the home listing. Fine for small buy-and-hold positions; poor for active trading or large size.

  3. Schwab Global Account (home-market listing). Schwab’s Global Account lets you trade the actual foreign listing online, in local currency, in 12 markets: Australia, Belgium, Canada, Finland, France, Germany, Hong Kong, Italy, Japan, Netherlands, Norway, and the U.K. 1 It’s a separate account (free to open/maintain), but you’ll pay local commissions (e.g., HK$250 per online Hong Kong trade, ~US$32), an FX conversion spread when converting USD↔︎local currency, trade during foreign market hours, and deal with foreign dividend withholding and the associated tax reporting.

WarningWhat is not easily accessible

Mainland China A-shares (Shanghai/Shenzhen — e.g., Sanhua’s 002050.SZ) are not in Schwab’s 12 Global markets and are generally not available to U.S. retail investors via Schwab. Sweden is also not among the 12 markets, so NIBE (Stockholm) can’t be bought via the Global Account — you’d need its U.S. OTC line (verify the current symbol). When a stock is only A-share or in an uncovered market, the OTC ADR is usually the only retail route.

Worked examples: Daikin and Sanhua

Daikin. Cleanest “real” exposure is the Tokyo line (6367) through the Schwab Global Account in yen; the low-friction route is the OTC ADR DKILY in your regular U.S. account. Note an ADR represents a set fraction of an ordinary share, so the ADR’s dollar price won’t equal the Tokyo share price — the economics are the same, the per-unit price differs by the ADR ratio (confirm the ratio on the ADR’s profile). Japan applies dividend withholding (treaty rate, typically 15% for U.S. holders).

Sanhua. Because the Shenzhen A-share (002050.SZ) isn’t accessible, your two realistic routes are: the Hong Kong H-share (2050) via the Schwab Global Account in HKD, or the OTC ADR (ZSICY) in your regular U.S. account. A nuance worth knowing: Sanhua is an “A+H” dual-listed company, and H-shares (Hong Kong) frequently trade at a different — often lower — price than the A-shares (Shenzhen), even though they’re the same company; the OTC ADR typically tracks one of those lines. Hong Kong does not withhold tax on dividends, which simplifies things versus some markets.

Is an ADR always the best route? Not quite

A reasonable rule of thumb is “use the U.S. ADR when one exists” — it’s the most convenient route (regular account, USD, normal hours). So: ADR is the right call for Daikin, irrelevant for Vertiv (just buy VRT), and the weakest for Sanhua — ZSICY is a recent, likely thinly traded OTC line, so check its live bid-ask spread and average volume, and use a limit order; if the spread looks wide, the Hong Kong shares (2050) via a Global Account may be the better fill. As a general practice for any OTC ADR (DKILY, ZSICY), use limit orders and note that unsponsored ADRs can carry small custody/pass-through fees.

WarningRetirement accounts (IRAs): the Global Account is not available

Schwab’s Global Account is “limited to individual and joint account registration types” — it cannot be opened under an IRA 2. In a retirement account you are therefore restricted to U.S.-listed shares and OTC ADRs, which all trade fine in an IRA: VRT, DKILY, ZSICY. The one place this bites is Sanhua: inside an IRA you can only use the ZSICY ADR, because the more-liquid Hong Kong line (2050) requires a Global Account. If ZSICY liquidity looks poor and you want Sanhua, that’s an argument for holding it in a taxable account (with a Global Account) instead of an IRA.

Opening a Schwab Global Account

If you decide you want a home-market listing (e.g., Sanhua 2050 in Hong Kong), the Global Account is an add-on linked to a taxable Schwab One brokerage account — not a standalone or retirement account:

  1. Apply. If you already have a Schwab One account, use the Schwab Global Account Add-On Application; if not, use the Combo Application (opens both at once). You can also call a Global Investing Specialist at 800-992-4685 or apply at a branch. 3
  2. No minimums or account fees — but you still pay per-trade local commissions (e.g., ~HK$250 for an online Hong Kong trade).
  3. Eligibility: individual and joint registrations only, U.S. residents living in the U.S. (so not IRAs).
  4. Fund and trade: move cash from your Schwab One, convert USD into the local currency (Schwab does the FX at a spread), then trade the foreign symbol in local currency during that market’s hours.

How to find the route for any other name here

For any foreign name in the notebook (Midea, Gree, Haier, Mitsubishi Electric, LG, Samsung, etc.): (1) check whether it has a U.S. OTC ADR (search the company name + “ADR” / OTC ticker), and (2) check whether its home market is one of Schwab’s 12. If neither, it’s effectively inaccessible to U.S. retail without a specialty broker. When in doubt, Schwab’s Global Investing desk (800-992-4685) can confirm availability and the exact order symbol.
