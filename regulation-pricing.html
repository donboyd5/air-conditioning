3  Regulation, standards, and pricing power

This chapter answers two questions you raised directly: (1) are U.S. efficiency standards (SEER2) eroding under the Trump administration, and does it matter for a long-term investor; and (2) how much have the SEER2 and refrigerant changes actually raised equipment prices — the “average selling price (ASP) uplift” that can show up as a structural revenue tailwind.

3.1 Are SEER2 standards eroding? Mostly no — but future tightening has stalled

The short version: the 2023 SEER2 minimum-efficiency standards are still in force and have not been rolled back. What the administration has done is freeze and challenge new and future rules, not repeal the existing floor. Three things are true as of mid-2026:

First, in February 2025 the DOE postponed the implementation dates of several Biden-era standards (touching central AC, water heaters, and other products) — a delay of future tightening, not a repeal of the 2023 baseline 1. In May 2025, Congress used the Congressional Review Act to permanently rescind standards for tankless gas water heaters, commercial refrigerators, and walk-in coolers — real repeals, but none of them residential AC/heat-pump SEER2 standards 2. Also in May 2025, DOE proposed a broad deregulatory package; it is proposed, contested, and faces near-certain litigation 3.

Second, there is a statutory backstop. The Energy Policy and Conservation Act contains an “anti-backsliding” provision that bars any new standard weaker than the one it replaces. This is why the existing SEER2 minimums are relatively safe; the practical effect of the current posture is that future tightening is frozen, not that today’s floor falls 4.

Third, ENERGY STAR survived. After signals in 2025 that it might be eliminated, industry pushback kept it funded (~$33M through FY2026) and moved it from EPA to DOE 5. Separately, federal incentives — notably heat-pump tax credits — are being wound down, which is a demand negative distinct from the standards themselves.

3.2 Does the regulatory stall matter for a 10–15 year investor? Largely no

Even if U.S. federal tightening stays frozen, three forces sustain demand for efficient equipment over your horizon:

The biggest is electricity prices. The EIA projects U.S. average residential power around 18¢/kWh in 2026, up from ~17.3¢ in 2025, and calls for the strongest multi-year electricity-demand growth since 2000 — driven heavily by data centers — with some regions (New England ~30¢, Pacific ~25¢) far above average 6. Rising bills make efficiency a consumer economic decision regardless of any mandate. Second, state standards and codes (California and others) and the broader electrification trend persist independently: U.S. heat pumps already outshipped gas furnaces in 2025 7. Third, the EU is tightening, not loosening — its revised F-Gas Regulation cuts HFC quotas hard and bans high-GWP refrigerants in new equipment on a faster schedule than the U.S., forcing the same manufacturers to keep investing in efficient, low-GWP designs 8.

Note

Investor takeaway. For a buy-and-hold horizon, treat the U.S. standards question as a second-order risk. The existing floor is protected by anti-backsliding; the durable demand drivers are rising electricity prices, state rules, EU regulation, and the economics of replacement — none of which depend on the federal administration tightening standards further. The clearer policy negative is the loss of U.S. heat-pump tax credits, which dampens near-term subsidy-driven demand.

3.3 The ASP uplift: how much did SEER2 + the refrigerant switch raise prices?

This is where regulation turns into a revenue tailwind. Two waves stacked:

The 2023 SEER2 step was widely reported to raise residential system prices by roughly 15–30%, but those figures blended the efficiency mandate with general post-2020 inflation, so attribution is imprecise. Some technical commentators argue the true engineering cost of SEER2 compliance was only a few percent, implying much of the increase was pricing power rather than cost pass-through — a claim now echoed in litigation (below).

The 2025 A2L refrigerant transition (R-410A → R-454B/R-32) has the cleanest number. Analysts at William Blair, based on distributor checks in early 2025, estimated A2L would raise HVAC equipment costs ~8–10%, and that the pricing was “sticking,” with A2L products reaching ~90% of equipment sales by spring 2025 9. Lennox publicly announced a ~10% price increase on its new R-454B residential products 10. On top of that, tariffs added an estimated further few percent.

Whether the uplift sticks as a multi-year tailwind is the key question for Carrier, Trane, and Lennox. The evidence so far says yes: HVAC equipment producer prices have risen far faster than general goods prices since 2020, and manufacturers held list prices through the 2025 volume downturn rather than discounting.

WarningA live overhang: antitrust litigation

The same pricing power that makes the ASP story attractive is now the subject of antitrust class actions. Beginning in early 2026, several price-fixing suits named Bosch, Carrier, Trane, Daikin, Lennox, Rheem, and AAON, alleging coordinated price increases and production cuts since 2020. All defendants deny the allegations, and the cases are unresolved — but this is a genuine, unquantified risk to the “ASP tailwind sticks” thesis and worth tracking 11.

3.4 Pre-buy / air-pocket: noise for your horizon

There was a clear 2024 pre-buy of R-410A equipment followed by a sharp 2025 destocking: combined AC + heat-pump shipments fell about 20% in 2025, and Carrier’s residential business fell ~40% in Q4 2025 as the channel worked off a multi-year inventory “overage” 12. For a 10–15 year investor this is a one-to-two-year inventory and transition distortion — it matters to 2025–2026 earnings optics and short-term traders, not to the long-run replacement-driven trajectory. I would only weight it heavily if it created a depressed entry valuation worth exploiting, or if the antitrust overhang above turned the “sticky pricing” into a liability.
